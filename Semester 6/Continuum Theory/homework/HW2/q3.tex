\section{Free Flow}
\begin{colbox}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Find the eulerian and lagrangian velocities of a non accelerated flow \(\ins{\frac{D}{Dt}v=0}\) in one dimension, given the initial condition \begin{equation}
            v(t=0)=-ax_0,\quad a>0
        \end{equation}
        What is the PDE solved by the eulerian velocity? Show that your solution satisfies it.
        \item What happens to the velocity at time \(t=a^{-1}\)? Explain both from the eulerian and lagrangian perspectives.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{colbox}
\subsection{non-accelerated flow}
For the lagrangian perspective we'll look at the lagrangian velocity at the initial condition
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x}(x_0, t=0) = -ax_0 \implies x(x_0,t) = -ax_0t+C
\end{equation}
I'm assuming I'm to treat \(x_0\) as the initial condition for the position, therefore 
\begin{equation}
    x(x_0,0) = C = x_0
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
    x(x_0,t) = -ax_0t+x_0 = x_0\ins{1-at}
\end{equation}
this lets us move to the eulerian picture by looking at the initial position in reference to the rest of the frame
\begin{equation}
    x_0 = \frac{x}{1-at}
\end{equation}
inserting this back into the lagrangian velocity
\begin{equation}
    v_E = \frac{-ax}{1-at}
\end{equation}
This solves the PDE
\begin{equation}
    \del{t}v+v\del{x}v=0
\end{equation}
we can show this by inserting the solution
\begin{equation}
    \del{t}\ins{\frac{-ax}{1-at}}+\ins{\frac{-ax}{1-at}}\del{x}\ins{\frac{-ax}{1-at}}=0
\end{equation}
simplifying
\begin{align}
    \frac{-a^2x}{\ins{1-at}^2}+\ins{\frac{-ax}{1-at}}\ins{\frac{-a}{1-at}}&=0\\
    \frac{-a^2x}{\ins{1-at}^2}+\frac{a^2x}{\ins{1-at}^2}&=0\\
    0&=0
\end{align}
\subsection{at that time}
plugging in \(t=a^{-1}\) into the position of the particle we get
\begin{equation}
    x(x_0,a^{-1})=x_0\ins{q-a\cdot a^{-1}}=0
\end{equation}
so in the lagrangian perspective the particle will be at position \(x=0\). In the eulerian perspective we can plug in the time and find 
\begin{equation}
    v_E = \frac{-ax}{1-a\cdot a^{-1}} = \text{undefined}
\end{equation}
If we combine the information from both perspectives, that the flow is entirely at \(x=0\) and that the velocity is undefined, that there needs to be an infinite velocity of the fluid to compress it all into the origin.
