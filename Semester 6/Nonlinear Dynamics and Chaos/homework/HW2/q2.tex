\section{Newton Method as an iterated map}
\begin{colbox}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Write down the Newton map explicitly for \(g(x) = x^2-4\). Show that it has exactly two fixed points that coincide with the roots of \(g\), and that both are superstable in the sense of the definition above.
        \item Starting from \(x_0=1\) iterate and report the first few iterates \(x_1,x_2,\dots\) until the result agrees with \(x^*=2\) to your tool's precision
        \item Return to a general twice differentiable \(g\) and let \(x^*\) be a root, \(g(x^*)=0\). Show that \(x^*\) is a superstable fixed point of the newton map if \(g'(x^*)\neq0\). What happens if \(g'(x^*)=0\)?
    \end{enumerate}
\end{colbox}
\subsection{Calibration}
Since
\begin{equation}
    g'(x) = 2x
\end{equation}
we have 
\begin{equation}
    f(x) = x-\frac{x^2-4}{2x}=\frac{x^2+4}{2x}=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{2}{x}
\end{equation}
to find the fixed points we look at 
\begin{equation}
    f(x)=x \implies \frac{x}{2}=\frac{2}{x} \implies x=\pm2
\end{equation}
the multiplier for these points is 
\begin{equation}
    f'(x) = \frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{x^2} \implies f'(\pm2)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}=0
\end{equation}
meaning both of the fixed points are superstable.
\subsection{Numerical Iteration}
Our series is 
\begin{equation}
    x_{n+1}=\frac{x_n}{2}+\frac{2}{x_n}
\end{equation}
so, using a simple python script
\begin{table}[!htb]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{c|c}
    \(n\) & \(x_n\) \\\hline
    0 & 1 \\
    1 & 2.5 \\
    2 & 2.05 \\
    3 & 2.000610 \\
    4 & 2.000000093 \\
    5 & 2.000000000000002 \\
    6 & 2
\end{tabular}
\caption{Iterates from \(x_0=1\)}
\end{table}
At iteration number 6 the value is so close to 2 that a 64-bit floating point number can no longer capture the difference.
\subsection{Generalisation}
For 
\begin{equation}
    f(x) = x-\frac{g(x)}{g'(x)}
\end{equation}
the multiplier is 
\begin{align}
    f'(x) &= 1-\frac{g'(x)^2-g(x)g''(x)}{g(x)^2} = \frac{g(x)g''(x)}{g'(x)^2}
\end{align}
it's immediate that if
\begin{align}
    g(x^*)=0 && g'(x^*)\neq0
\end{align}
then 
\begin{equation}
    f'(x^*)=0
\end{equation}
making \(x^*\) superstable. If instead \(g'(x^*)=0\) then the Newton map formula is undefined. Instead if the root has multiplicity \(m>1\) then we can remove the singularity and get \(f'(x^*)=\frac{m-1}{m}\) making the root attracing but not superstable.
