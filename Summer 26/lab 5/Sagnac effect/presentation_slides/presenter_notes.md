**Uncertainty notation.** A number like 0.02039(4) means 0.02039 ± 0.00004. The digit in parentheses is the uncertainty in the last digit shown. All uncertainties are one standard deviation. On the slides every uncertainty is rounded to one significant digit and the value to the same digit. The comparisons (σ and p values) are always computed from the unrounded numbers, which are given below.

**χ²/dof and p (used on several slides).** For a fit, χ² = Σ (data − fit)² / u², summed over the points, where u is each point's uncertainty. If the uncertainties are right and the model fits, χ² is about equal to the number of degrees of freedom, dof = number of points − number of fitted parameters, so χ²/dof ≈ 1. The p value is the probability of getting a χ² at least this large if the model and the uncertainties were correct. A tiny p (for example 10⁻⁸) means the model, or the error bars, are not enough to describe the data.

**SEM** (backup slides B2, B3, and the slide 8 notes) is the standard error of the mean: the standard deviation of the photos at one setting divided by √(number of photos). It is how well the mean of those photos is known, if the photos are independent.

## 02 Rotation delays the beam that travels with it (1:00). Let the animation run once.

**Say:** "A beam splitter on the table sends light both ways around a closed loop. While the light travels, the splitter moves with the table. The beam going with the rotation (blue) has to chase the splitter, so it needs a little more than one full turn. The beam going against the rotation (orange) meets the splitter early. The bar at the bottom shows the two return times and the gap Δt between them. Why does the camera see a delay? Both beams carry the same laser light, a wave with period T. Delaying one by Δt shifts its wave by Δt/T of a period, a phase shift of 2πΔt/T, and that moves the bright and dark bands where the two beams recombine."

**The formulas, step by step:**

1. **Travel times.** In the lab frame both beams move at c. The splitter moves at v = ωR. The co-rotating beam must cover the loop length l plus the distance the splitter moved, so t₊ = l/(c − v). The counter-rotating beam needs t₋ = l/(c + v). [Pascoli Eq. 1, Manual "Classical derivation"]
2. **Delay.** Δt = t₊ − t₋ = 2lv/(c² − v²). For a circle, l = 2πR and v = ωR. Since v ≪ c this becomes Δt ≈ 4Aω/c² with A = πR². With N turns, each enclosing the area A of one turn, the delay is N times larger: Δt = 4NAω/c². [Manual Eq. 1–2, Tartaglia Eq. 1–2, Pascoli Eq. 2, Schwartz Eq. 2–3]
3. **Phase (the wave picture on the slide).** Both beams carry light of one period T = λ/c. At the splitter, one beam's field is cos(2πt/T) and the delayed one's cos(2π(t − Δt)/T): they differ by the phase 2πΔt/T. The camera records their interference, brightness ∝ 1 + cos(phase difference), so a change of the phase difference moves the bright and dark bands. So Δφ = (2π/T)Δt = (2πc/λ)Δt = 8πNAω/(λc). This is the Sagnac formula, the equation we test. [Manual Eq. 3–5, Schwartz Eq. 1, Pascoli Eq. 3]

**Where relativity is (it is not on the slides).** The only assumption in step 1 is that light moves at c in both directions in the lab frame, whatever the motion of the splitter. That is Einstein's second postulate. The effect is first order in v/c, so the classical derivation and special relativity give the same Δt. Relativity changes it only at order (v/c)², about 10⁻¹⁷ for our rim speed of about 1 m/s [Pascoli Sec. 3, Langevin's derivation, "an experiment of the first order in v/c", Tartaglia "to lowest order in v/c", Manual introduction: "a fully relativistic effect"]. Relativity does matter in the fiber, see "Other questions" at the end.

**Subtitle.** Optical gyroscopes ("gyrolasers", used since the 1970s on planes, ships and submarines) measure rotation rates with this effect [Manual introduction, Tartaglia Sec. I, Schwartz Sec. 1].

**The size of the effect (not on this slide, say it on slide 5).** λ and fringes have not been introduced yet, so the slide gives no numbers. At the top speed, ω = 1.81 rad/s: Δt = 4·13·(1.02 m²)·(1.81 rad/s)/c² = 1.07 fs. One light period at 640 nm is λ/c = 2.13 fs, so the delay is half a period and the predicted phase is 3.15 rad, half a fringe. We measured 3.29 ± 0.06 rad (slide 12).

**Not to scale.** The animation draws the rim moving at 0.25c so the effect is visible, and a single turn instead of 13. With that speed the return times are 2π/(1 − 0.25) and 2π/(1 + 0.25) in units of R/c. In the experiment the rim moves at only about 1 m/s, about 3×10⁻⁹ c.

## 03 Both beams travel the same 13-turn fiber loop (0:45)

**Say:** "The laser goes through a polarizer to the beam splitter. One beam is coupled into the fiber at L₁, the other at L₂. They travel the same 13 turns in opposite directions, recombine at the splitter and go to the camera. Everything you see turns with the table. We measure two things: ω with a stopwatch, and Δφ from photographs of the fringes."

- The drawing is based on the setup figure of the lab manual (Fig. 1 on p. 12, under "Minimum experimental tasks"). L₁ and L₂ couple the light into and out of the fiber, L₃ is the lens in front of the camera. The polarizers reduce unwanted reflections [Prep Q6, Manual prep question 3].
- The same figure also shows a second, small loop (R = 15.5 ± 0.1 cm, 46 ± 1 turns) in the top-left quarter, used for task 4. The drawing leaves it out because we did not measure with it: task 4 was skipped on the instructor's instruction (see "Other questions").
- **System parameters** N = 13 ± 1, R = 57.0 ± 0.5 cm and λ = 640 nm are given in the caption of that figure [Manual Fig. 1, p. 12].
- **Fiber type (the slide says SMF-28e+).** The manual's text calls the fiber SMF-28e (the V-number section, the heading of its specification page and its preparation task). The specification sheet printed under that heading is Corning's SMF-28e+ datasheet, and the preparation sheet also says SMF-28e+ (Prep Q11–Q12). The a = 4.1 µm and NA = 0.14 used everywhere come from that datasheet. If asked: nothing in our analysis depends on which name is right.
- **Prediction.** Equation 5 is linear in ω, Δφ = a_th·ω, with slope a_th = 8πNA/(λc). The loop is a circle, so A = πR² = 1.02 m² (the gray line under the formula) and a_th = 8π²NR²/(λc) = 1.738 ± 0.137, shown rounded as 1.7 ± 0.1 rad/(rad/s) [Manual Eq. 5]. The comparisons on later slides use the unrounded values. The unit rad/(rad/s) is radians of phase per rad/s of rotation (it equals seconds).
- **Where ±0.137 comes from.** Only N and R carry uncertainties. N = 13 ± 1 is 7.7 %. R enters squared, so its 0.5/57.0 = 0.9 % counts twice, 1.8 %. Together √(7.7² + 1.8²) = 7.9 %, and 7.9 % of 1.738 is 0.137. λ was given without an uncertainty and c₀ is exact.
- **If asked where the ±0.1 comes from** (not on the slide, say it verbally): "Almost all of it is the turn count, 13 ± 1, which is 7.7 %. The radius adds 1.8 %." The same two numbers appear again in the budget on slide 11.
- *If asked where 13 ± 1 comes from.* The manual gives it (Fig. 1 caption, p. 12). The turns were not counted by us. We treat ±1 as one standard deviation. If it is a bound (13 for sure within ±1), the standard uncertainty of a flat distribution is 1/√3 = 0.58 turns, and the uncertainty of c would drop from 8.7 % to about 6 %. Keeping ±1 is the cautious choice. R is the given loop radius, taken as the mean radius of the windings.

## 04 A stopwatch calibration converts motor setting to ω (0:50)

**Say:** "The motor controller gives a setting S, not an angular velocity. At nine settings we timed five to eight full turns with a stopwatch, converted the mean turn time to ω = 2π/T and fitted a straight line." [Manual minimum task 1]

- **Motor setting S.** The manual's task 1 asks for the (linear) ratio between the voltage on the motor and its angular velocity [Manual minimum task 1]. S is the number set on the motor controller (no unit, the "Speed" column of our calibration file), and it sets the motor voltage. We calibrated ω directly against S, as the slide's subtitle says.
- **Right, bottom: one point in detail.** At S = 90 we timed 8 turns. Each dot is one turn time from the stopwatch splits. The mean is ⟨T⟩ = 3.545 s, so ω = 2π/⟨T⟩ = 1.772 rad/s. This is the top point of the main plot.
- **Fit result.** k = 0.02039(4) rad/s per unit of S, that is 0.02039 ± 0.00004. The intercept is ω₀ = −0.0212(6) rad/s, if asked. We do not show it because the line was only tested inside the timed range (S = 5.78 to 90), so its value at S = 0 means nothing.
- **χ²/dof = 47.2/7, p = 5×10⁻⁸.** The line does not describe the points within their error bars. The points bend slightly: at the top setting the table turns 2.3 % slower than the line predicts. We use the linear relation the manual asks for. A quadratic would change the slope by 1.7 % (backup B1), and that 1.7 % is part of the final uncertainty (slide 9).
- **"points within 2.3 % of the line, error bars 0.5 % of ω" (how to defend the χ²).** The line is rejected because the timing is very precise, not because it is far off. In order of S the points deviate by −0.6, +1.3, +0.8, +0.7, +1.1, +0.7, −0.4, −1.3, −2.3 %, a bend that a quadratic removes (χ²/dof = 6.0/6). Say: "The stopwatch is precise enough to see that the table turns about 2 % slower than linear at the top. We use the line, as the task asks, and carry what a curved calibration would change, 1.7 % on the slope, as an uncertainty. It cannot change the agreement." Do not offer R² instead: it is 0.9991 for this rejected line, because R² ignores the error bars.
- **Why χ² here and not R².** Here every point has its own error bar from the stopwatch, so χ² can test the line against those error bars, which is the stronger test. R² only says how much of the variation the line accounts for. For this calibration R² would be above 0.999 even though the line is rejected, because R² does not know how small the error bars are. On slide 8 the situation is the other way around: the points have no trustworthy error bars of their own, so the regression takes the noise from the data, χ²/dof is 1 by construction there, and R² is the useful summary.
- **Error bars (not on the slide, if asked).** Each point has two relative uncertainties, u(ω)/ω, combined in quadrature. Together they are at most 0.5 % of ω, smaller than the markers, which is why no bars are visible.
  - *Stopwatch timing.* The turns were taken as stopwatch splits (one press of "lap" per pass), so neighboring turns share a button press. The mean turn time is (t_n − t₀)/n: only the first and last presses matter. If each press has an independent error σ_r, the mean has variance 2σ_r²/n², while the turn times have E[SD²] = 2σ_r²(n+1)/n. Eliminating σ_r gives u(T) = SD/√(n(n+1)) [`calibration.py` header, Analysis README "Calibration"]. A check that the turns really are splits: neighboring turn times should be anticorrelated (a late press makes one turn long and the next short). They are: −0.34, against −0.43 ± 0.09 expected for splits [`calibration.txt`]. Since ω = 2π/T, u(ω)/ω = u(T)/T. At S = 90 the eight turn times have SD = 0.054 s, so u(T) = 0.054/√72 = 0.006 s and u(ω)/ω = 0.18 %. Over the nine settings this is 0.04–0.18 %.
  - *Speed reproducibility ε = 0.48 %.* If the table is set to the same S again, its real speed differs by about 0.48 % of ω. We do not measure this directly. It is the extra spread of the settings about a smooth curve that the stopwatch alone cannot explain [Analysis README, "Calibration"]. It is added in quadrature to every error bar and later to every ω.
- The dashed vertical lines are the nine settings used for the photographs.
- **Footer: 1.81 against 1.772.** The photos get their ω from the straight line. At S = 90 the line gives 1.81 rad/s, while the timed turns at that setting give 1.772 rad/s, 2.3 % less. That is why 1.81 appears on later slides. The quadratic calibration, which follows the timed points, gives 1.78, and the resulting change of the slope (1.7 %) is in the final uncertainty (slide 9, backup B1).

## 06 The rings are evenly spaced in r², not in r (1:00)

**Say:** "To measure the rings in every photo, we send 170 rays out from the center and find where each dark ring is lowest along each ray: these are the colored dots. Then we fit concentric circles to all the dots at once. The circles give the center and the radius of every ring. And the radii hold a surprise: the rings are not evenly spaced in r. Against r² they are, with one constant spacing P. That is what two beams with different wavefront radii give: their phase difference grows in proportion to r², and the dark rings sit where it reaches π, 3π, 5π. And this is why rotation moves them inward: rotation adds the same phase everywhere, so every dark ring has to move to a smaller r² to stay at π, 3π, 5π. The pattern keeps its shape and slides inward."

**The ring finder (left).** [Analysis, `fringes.py` steps 1–4]
- The red channel is smoothed with a Gaussian of σ = 4 px against speckle. Only the red channel carries light.
- 170 rays every 1.5° from −75° to 180°. The lower-left part of the pattern has no fringes, so it is left out.
- Along each ray, each dark ring's minimum is searched within ±0.3 of the local fringe period around a first guess, and refined to below a pixel with a parabola through the neighboring samples. Shallow minima (noise) are dropped.
- Concentric circles (one center, one radius per ring) are fitted to all minima with a robust loss, so a few stray points from speckle have little pull. The rays are then re-centered on the new center and the search is repeated, three rounds in all.
- The dots are the minima of the last round, colored by ring. The dashed circles are the fit, labeled m = 0, 1, 2 from the center out.

**How good the circle fit is (label on the photo, and the footer).**
- *"circle fit, rms 6 px"* (bottom left of the photo) is the root mean square distance of the single minima (the dots) from their fitted circles in this photo. Over all 161 photos it is 2.8–8.2 px, median 4.7 px [Check, `fringes_frames.csv`, column rms_px]. This is the speckle on single minima. It is small next to the 130–240 px between neighboring rings.
- *"radii typically ±1 px"* is the uncertainty of each fitted ring radius, from the fit. Each ring has many minima (median 136, at least 54), so its radius is known much better than a single dot: 0.3–2.3 px over all photos, median 0.8 px. The center is known to about ±0.6 px [Check, `fringes_frames.csv`, columns uR1–uR3, n1–n3, u_cx, u_cy].
- *If asked why no χ²:* the single minima have no independent error bars, so the fit uses their measured distance from the circles (the rms) as their noise. That rms is the fit quality, and the ±1 px follows from it.

**Why rings, and why r² (right).**
- Two plane waves meeting at an angle give straight, evenly spaced fringes with period Λ = λ/(2 sin θ), where θ is each beam's angle to the axis [Manual Eq. 6 and Fig. 2, Prep Q1–Q2]. Our beams are not plane waves. They leave the fiber as Gaussian beams.
- A Gaussian beam has the transverse phase kρ²/(2R(z)), where R(z) is the wavefront radius [QE3, "The phase of the Gaussian Beam" slide, PDF p. 10, Prep Q3].
- The two output beams reach the camera with different wavefront radii R₁ and R₂. Their phase difference is then (k r²/2)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) plus a constant. Writing k = 2π/λ gives φ(r) = 2π r²/P + φ_c with P = 2λ/|1/R₁ − 1/R₂| [Analysis, `fringes.py` header "Why rings"].
- **Why the rings move inward (not on the slide, say it).** The dark rings sit where φ = π, 3π, 5π, at R_m² = P(m + η). Rotation adds the same Δφ at every point (φ_c → φ_c + Δφ), so to stay at π, 3π, 5π every ring moves to a smaller r² by the same step, R_m² → R_m² − PΔφ/2π. Our half fringe (3.3 rad) moves the first dark ring from 258 px to 101 px. Analogy if useful: the rings are contour lines of a bowl, and rotation fills the bowl evenly, so every contour shrinks toward the center and none appears or disappears.
- **What P is.** Going out by P in r² adds 2π to the phase, one full fringe. So neighboring dark rings are P apart in r², and P is the same for every ring. It is set only by the two wavefront radii. Median over all photographs P = 1.07×10⁵ px².
- So the phase grows linearly in r², and the dark rings are equally spaced in r² with period P. The plots show it: 241 px then 132 px between dark rings against r, but 1.08 × 10⁵ px² both times against r². Schwartz makes the same observation: the pattern is not linear like Young's fringes, and fringe widths differ between the center and the edge [Schwartz Sec. 3].
- **What moves the rings.** Rotation adds the same phase across the whole beam. That changes only φ_c, so the rings slide inward or outward. A relative tilt of the beams mainly moves the ring center. A change of a lens–fiber distance mainly changes the wavefront radii, so it changes P [Analysis, `fringes.py` header, Prep Q7 asks about this]. This is why we track φ_c. Both are only approximately free of phase: a tilt about a pivot away from the center also shifts φ_c a little, and refocusing changes the Gouy phase of the beam on the axis [QE3, PDF p. 10, the on-axis phase kz − ζ(z)]. Effects like these are a plausible part of the wander between settings (slide 8 notes). (The tilt and refocus sentence used to be on the slide. It is now only here, if asked.)
- The profiles on the right are the intensity averaged over the fringe sector in thin rings around the fitted center.

## 07 Squared ring radii give the phase at the center (0:50)

**Say:** "For every photo we now have the ring radii. Plotting R² against the ring number gives a straight line. Its intercept tells us how far the innermost ring is from the center, in units of one fringe, and that is the phase at the center."

**Formulas.**
- From slide 6, φ(r) = 2π r²/P + φ_c. A dark ring is where the two beams cancel, φ = π + 2πm [Manual Eq. 6 with cos = −1, Prep Q1]. Solving for r² gives R_m² = P(m + η) with η = ½ − φ_c/2π [Analysis, `fringes.py` step 5].
- So a straight line of R_m² against m has slope P and intercept Pη. In practice: fit R_m² = Pm + b, then η = b/P and φ_c = π − 2πη. The plot shows R_m²/P, where the slope is 1 by construction and the intercept is η.
- *If asked about the rest photos.* At rest only two rings are inside the fringe region, so the line goes exactly through both points and cannot be checked by itself. At the three lowest speeds some photos also have only two, and from S = 44 on every photo has three. η stays between 0 and 1 during the whole run (0.62 to 0.10), so the ring numbering never has to be shifted [Check, `fringes_frames.csv`].
- **This pair.** η goes from 0.62 at rest to 0.10 at the top speed. The phase change is Δφ = 2π × 0.52 ≈ 3.3 rad.
- This is the manual's method (shift of each minimum divided by the local fringe period, averaged over the minima) [Manual Eq. 7] and Schwartz's Δφ = 2π Δx/δ [Schwartz Eq. 5], done in r². In r² every ring has the same period P, so one straight-line fit uses all the rings at once.
- **Why it is robust.** P is fitted for each photo, so a change of the ring spacing does not move φ_c. The positions of minima also do not depend on the camera's brightness response [Analysis README].

**Is a pixel-to-length calibration needed? No.** The phase comes from η = b/P, the ratio of two numbers that are both in px². If one pixel were some length s on the camera, both b and P would be multiplied by s², and η would not change. So the pixel size and the camera magnification never enter Δφ, and the slide works in pixels throughout. A length calibration would only be needed to turn P into the physical wavefront radii R₁ and R₂, which the experiment does not need. (The slide used to mention this. It is now only here.)

## 08 The phase grows linearly with ω, as Sagnac predicts (1:00)

**Say:** "Each point is the mean phase of one setting, against its calibrated ω. A linear regression, a straight-line fit, gives a slope of 1.84 ± 0.02 rad per rad/s. The prediction from the loop geometry is 1.7 ± 0.1. The small plot on the right shows both with their error bars: they overlap. The difference is 0.7 standard deviations."

- **Why 1.84 and 1.7 agree although they look far apart.** Both are rounded. Unrounded, a = 1.839 ± 0.024 and a_th = 1.738 ± 0.137. The difference is 0.101, not the 0.14 the rounded numbers suggest. The two uncertainties combine as √(0.024² + 0.137²) = 0.139. So the difference is z = 0.101/0.139 = 0.72 standard deviations, shown as 0.7σ. The two-sided p = 0.47 is the probability of a difference at least this large (in either direction) if the two values were the same and the uncertainties right, from the normal distribution, p = 2(1 − Φ(0.72)). It is not the probability that theory is right. The prediction's error bar is wide because of N = 13 ± 1 (slide 3 notes).
- **Fit.** Δφ = aω + b, an ordinary linear regression (least squares) through the ten setting means, all with equal weight. This is how Schwartz obtains his slopes: "fitting a linear regression model to the data (Δφ = a·Ω + b)", quoting the regression's standard errors [Schwartz Sec. 3, Eq. 7].
- **Where the slope error comes from (in plain words).** A regression does not need error bars on the points. It measures how far the points lie from the line and uses that as the uncertainty of every point: s = √(Σ (Δφ − line)² / (10 − 2)) = 0.045 rad. The 10 − 2 is like the N − 1 of an ordinary standard deviation: two numbers (slope and intercept) were already taken from the same ten points. The slope's standard error follows from s and the spread of the ω values: u_a = s/√(Σ(ω − ω̄)²) = 0.024 [standard least-squares result, as in Schwartz]. Adding the calibration line's 0.004 in quadrature gives 0.024 again, shown as ± 0.02.
- **R² = 0.9986.** The fraction of the variation of Δφ that the straight line accounts for. 1 would mean every point exactly on the line. Why R² and not χ² here: in a regression the noise is taken from the points themselves, so χ²/dof = 1 by construction and tells nothing. (On slide 4 the points have their own error bars, so χ² is used there.)
- **Why not use the photos' own error bars.** Weighting each setting by the SEM of its photos (0.004–0.028 rad) plus the table-speed term gives χ²/dof = 56.6/8, p = 2×10⁻⁹: the points sit 2.7 times farther from the line than those error bars allow. The SEM assumes that the photos of one setting are independent, and they are not, because the phase wanders slowly during the run (backup B4) [`sagnac_update/sagnac.txt`]. The distance of the points from the line contains every effect that moves a setting's mean (photo noise, table speed, wander), so the regression uses it directly. Do not regress all 161 photos instead: that would treat correlated photos as independent and give 1.85 ± 0.01, which looks more precise than it is [Check].
- **If asked why the line misses the origin.** The fitted intercept is −0.05 ± 0.03 rad, 1.8 standard errors from zero (p = 0.11), and the rest point sits 0.05 rad above the line. Sagnac predicts zero, but the rest value is itself uncertain: it is the mean of 10 photos taken at the start, and setting means wander by about 0.045 rad. Forcing the line through the origin gives a = 1.80 ± 0.01. That 0.04 difference is smaller than the method uncertainty on the next slide, and both values agree with the prediction [Check].

## 09 Three full-image methods differ by at most 6 % (0:50)

**Say:** "Does the result depend on how we read the phase from the photos? We applied more methods to the same photographs. The three that use the whole image differ by at most 6 %. That spread is our method uncertainty. Together with the fit and the calibration this gives a = 1.84 ± 0.07."

- **Cosine fit** [Prep Q5 asks for two ways to extract Δφ]. The intensity against s = r² is fitted with C e^(−s/s_C) + B e^(−s/s_B) cos(2πs/P + φ) + D. The exponentials are the envelopes of two Gaussian beams of different size [QE3, intensity slide, PDF p. 7], D is the dark level, φ is the phase at the center [Analysis README]. It uses every pixel, but relies on the envelope model and on a linear camera response.
- **Template matching.** Each r² profile is shifted and scaled to match the averaged rest profile. The shift D in r² gives the phase 2πD/P [Analysis README]. It uses no fringe model, but reads the phase in the middle of the comparison window rather than at the center.
- **10 random pixels** (instructor's method, slide 10). The row shows the mean over 1000 random draws of ten pixels, 1.70, not one particular draw. Its error bar is 0.06, the typical regression error of a single draw. Why not the 0.006 uncertainty of the mean of 1000 draws: all draws use the same 161 photographs, so the wander of the setting means is common to every draw and does not average out. The 0.006 only says how well the average over pixel choices is known for this one data set. 0.06 is the conservative choice. Either way the pixel row lies 0.14 below the ring-minima row. Slide 10 explains part of that difference.
- **Left panel.** Each full-image method's phase change minus the ring-minima phase change, per setting. The cosine fit reads 0.1–0.19 rad higher at every moving setting. The difference comes from the rest photos, where the bright flat center does not look like a cosine (fit rms 9.5 counts at rest, against 1.4–2.3 at high speed) [`sagnac_update/sagnac.txt`].
- **Right panel.** The slopes 1.84, 1.88 and 1.77, each from the same linear regression as slide 8, with regression and calibration-line error bars. "At most 6 %": the three full-image slopes span 1.77–1.88, 0.11 apart, 6 % of 1.84. Their error bars do not all overlap, which is exactly why the difference is counted as a method uncertainty. The dashed line separates the pixel method, which is not used for the result.
- **The gray band is the prediction ± 1σ, 1.738 ± 0.137 (1.60–1.88).** Its width comes only from the given N and R: 7.7 % from N = 13 ± 1 and 1.8 % from R (squared, 2 × 0.9 %), √(7.7² + 1.8²) = 7.9 % of 1.738. λ has no given uncertainty and c₀ is exact (slide 3 notes).
- **Method uncertainty.** These methods read the same photographs, so they are correlated checks, not independent measurements. Their differences show how much the answer depends on the choice of method. We take half the range of the three full-image slopes, (1.876 − 1.768)/2 = 0.054 rad/(rad/s), about 3 %, as a systematic uncertainty [Analysis README]. The pixel method is not used for it: its spread between draws (0.2) is far larger than the differences between the other methods, and slide 10 shows that it is biased low by its fixed pixel positions.
- **The bold line.** Shown on the slide as percentages of a (1.3 %, 3.0 %, 1.7 %, together 3.7 %), the same rows as the budget on slide 11. Three parts: 0.024 regression (with the calibration line), 0.054 method, 0.032 calibration model (the 1.7 % change between a linear and a quadratic calibration, backup B1). √(0.024² + 0.054² + 0.032²) = 0.067, so a = 1.84 ± 0.07 rad/(rad/s). Our written report (`sagnac_update/RESULTS.md`) quotes 1.84 ± 0.06, because it lists the calibration-model change separately instead of adding it. The deck adds it, hence ± 0.07.
- **Why add them in quadrature (and how it could be attacked).** Uncertainties from independent sources add in quadrature, because their variances add. The three come from different things: the noise between settings (regression), the choice of how to read the phase (method), and the choice of calibration curve (model). They are close to independent: the three methods share the same photos and calibration, so the regression noise is mostly common to them and drops out of their differences, and the calibration model changes ω for all methods alike, so it does not enter the method range. Expected questions:
  1. *"Half the range as one standard deviation is arbitrary."* It is a convention, not a derived result. Alternatives: range/√12 (a flat distribution over the range) gives 0.031, the full range 0.108. With the full range the total becomes 0.115 and the difference from theory 0.6σ.
  2. *"Why not add them linearly?"* A linear sum, 0.024 + 0.054 + 0.032 = 0.110, assumes all three push the same way at full size, the worst case. Then a = 1.84 ± 0.11 and the difference from theory is 0.6σ. No choice changes the conclusion, because the prediction's own ± 0.14 is the largest uncertainty.
  3. *"Is the regression error counted twice?"* Each method's slope has its own regression error, and the method term is built from differences between slopes, so a little regression noise may also sit in the method term. If so, the total is slightly too large, which is the cautious direction.
- **Footer.** Each method's regression R² (0.9986, 0.9982, 0.9993 from the top), the same fit-quality number as on slide 8.
- **If asked.** Each method's points lie typically 0.045, 0.053 and 0.031 rad from its line. With each method's own photo-to-photo errors (SEM) and the table-speed term instead, every method's line is rejected: χ²/dof = 56.6/8, 131.6/8 and 42.7/8, p = 2×10⁻⁹, 1×10⁻²⁴ and 1×10⁻⁶ [Check]. So every method sees the same extra variation between settings.

## 10 The slope from ten random pixels depends on the draw (0:45, optional)

**Say:** "This is the instructor's method: pick ten pixels in the fringe region and follow their brightness through all photos. As the rings move, each pixel gets brighter and darker, and one fit finds the phase of every photo from all ten pixels together. The question is how much the answer depends on which ten pixels you pick. We repeated the whole method a thousand times with different random pixels."

- **Model.** I_jk = a_j + b_j cos(ψ_j + δ_k): pixel j in photo k has an offset a_j and an amplitude b_j, ψ_j = 2π s_j/P₀ is its fringe phase at rest (s_j its r² from the rest ring center, P₀ the rest period) and δ_k is the photo's phase change. An alternating least-squares fit finds all a_j, b_j and δ_k, with the physical limits b_j ≥ 0 and a_j − b_j ≥ dark level [Analysis, `sagnac_update/methods.py` header, `methods.txt`]. This is the same cosine law of two-beam interference as Manual Eq. 6. Each draw's slope comes from the same averaging per setting and the same line as the analysis [Scan].
- **The scan.** 1000 draws of ten pixels (seeds 1–1000), each fitted exactly like the draw in our analysis [Scan]. That draw (seed 20261011) gave 1.79, well inside the histogram. It is no longer marked on the slide, because the mean over draws is what slide 9 uses.
- **The table.**
  - *Mean slope 1.702(6).* The average of the 998 good draws. The (6) is how well this average is known from 998 draws, SD/√998.
  - *Spread (SD) 0.196(5).* The standard deviation of the 998 slopes, how much one draw differs from another. The (5) is how well this SD is known, from resampling the draws (bootstrap). The SD is the plain textbook measure. A robust measure that ignores outliers (half the width of the middle 68 % of the draws) gives 0.187, almost the same, so the choice does not matter.
  - *Typical fit error 0.057.* The median of the slope errors that each draw's own regression reports.
  - *Spread ÷ fit error 3.4.* One draw's error bar suggests ±0.06, but the draws actually differ by ±0.2. So one draw of ten pixels gives the slope only to about ±0.2, about 11 %, three times worse than its own error bar says.
- **Failures (footer).** 2 of the 1000 draws gave a negative slope. These fits failed and are left out (they lie off the plot). 18 draws stopped at the 500-round limit of the fit before converging. Their slopes are kept. Ten pixels that each see only half a fringe of motion (the fitted phases span 3.2 rad, `methods.txt`) cannot always separate a phase change from a change of contrast, which is why the fit needs the limits on a_j and b_j.
- **Purple outline (diagnostic).** The same pixels, but each pixel's ψ_j computed from that photo's own fitted ring center and period instead of the rest values. The mean moves from 1.70 to 1.78 and the spread narrows a little (SD 0.17). So about half of the 0.14 difference from the ring minima comes from the second limitation on the slide: the rings' center and period change slightly during the run, and a pixel at a fixed position reads that as a phase change.
- **"Limitations in our run" (first point).** Our largest shift is half a fringe (0.52), so each pixel goes through less than one bright-dark cycle. The fit then has to tell a phase change from a change of brightness or contrast with very little to go on, which is why one draw depends so much on its pixels. This is a property of our run, not of the method: with a shift of several fringes each pixel would pass through full cycles. That is the question Prep Q5 asks ("assuming that Δφ_max is large, which way is more accurate?"). For a large shift, following the brightness of fixed points works well. Our shift is small, where the ring positions do better.
- *If asked "the pixel method gives 1.70, closer to the prediction 1.74 than your 1.84".* The prediction's ± 0.14 cannot tell 1.70 from 1.84: both are within one standard deviation of it. The point of the slide is not which mean is closer, but that one draw of ten pixels gives any value from about 1.5 to 1.9 (± 0.2), while each draw's own error bar says ± 0.06.
- **More pixels per draw** (60 draws each for 20, 40 and 80 pixels). Spread (SD) 0.196, 0.091, 0.073 and 0.056 for 10, 20, 40 and 80 pixels, while one draw's fit error is 0.057, 0.041, 0.040 and 0.037. The spread shrinks with more pixels but stays larger than the fit error, and the mean settles at about 1.73 (1.70, 1.71, 1.73, 1.73), below the ring-minima 1.84. More pixels reduce the luck of the draw but not the bias from fixed positions.
- **Why the ring-minima method avoids this.** It uses whole rings and fits the center and the period in every photo (slides 6 and 7).

## 11 The slope gives the speed of light, 0.7σ from c₀ (0:45)

We get (2.8 ± 0.2) × 10⁸ m/s, within one standard deviation of the defined value. The uncertainty is 8.7 %, and most of it comes from the turn count. Not included is a possible steady drift of the phase in time."

If asked for the numbers: c = 8π²·13·(0.570 m)²/(640 nm · 1.839 s) = 2.83 × 10⁸ m/s, with an uncertainty of 0.246 × 10⁸ m/s, rounded to (2.8 ± 0.2) × 10⁸ m/s. Note that rad/(rad/s) is a time, so a is in seconds. This is c inferred through the Sagnac formula, not a direct time-of-flight measurement.

- **Comparison.** c/c₀ = 0.95 ± 0.08, 0.7σ from the defined value, two-sided p = 0.50. (Slide 8 gives p = 0.47 because there only the fit uncertainty of a is used. Here the method and calibration-model parts are included too.)
- **Uncertainty budget.** Relative uncertainties add in quadrature: (u_c/c)² = (u_a/a)² + (u_N/N)² + (2u_R/R)², with u_a containing the method, calibration-model and regression parts [`sagnac_update/sagnac.txt` for the form]. The reasons for adding in quadrature are in the slide 9 notes.

  - Phase-reading method: 3.0 % (slide 9).
  - Loop radius: R enters squared, so its 0.9 % uncertainty counts twice, 1.8 %.
  - Calibration model: 1.7 %, the change between a linear and a quadratic calibration (backup B1).
  - Regression and calibration line: 1.3 % (regression 1.3 %, calibration line 0.2 %).
  - Total of the listed sources 8.7 %. Everything from our own measurement (fit, calibration, calibration model, method) is 3.7 %. The given loop geometry is the rest.
- **Not included (on the slide).** A steady drift of the phase in time: the speeds were taken in increasing order, so it cannot be separated from the slope (slide 12, backup B4). The wavelength: λ was given without an uncertainty. Each nanometer would change c by 0.16 % [`sagnac_update/sagnac.txt`].
- **Turn count.** Read the other way, if c, R and λ are taken as known, the measured slope fixes N = 13.8 ± 0.5 turns (the 3.7 % of a), about twice as precise as the given 13 ± 1. Including R's uncertainty it is ± 0.6. `sagnac_update/sagnac.txt` quotes ± 0.3 because it leaves out the method and calibration-model parts [Check].
- This is the same comparison as slide 8 (c/c₀ = a_th/a), shown as a speed of light. It is not an independent second test.

## 12 The Sagnac phase is linear in ω and matches theory (0:30)

**Say the three numbers:**
1. The slope of Δφ against ω is 1.84 ± 0.07 rad/(rad/s), with the full uncertainty (regression and calibration line, method, calibration model). The prediction 8π²NR²/(λc) = 1.7 ± 0.1 is 0.7σ away (0.101 apart, combined uncertainty √(0.067² + 0.137²) = 0.15).
2. The largest shift is 3.29 ± 0.06 rad, half a fringe, at ω = 1.81 rad/s. The ± 0.06: Δφ there is the difference of two setting means (top speed minus rest), each uncertain by about the 0.045 rad the points lie from the line, so √2 × 0.045 ≈ 0.06. In time, Δt = Δφ λ/(2πc) = 1.1 fs. This is inferred from the phase, not timed.
3. Read as a speed of light, c = (2.8 ± 0.2) × 10⁸ m/s, 0.7σ from the defined value, limited mainly by the turn count.

**Limitation.** A slow, steady drift of the phase in time would change the slope (up or down, depending on its sign), because the speeds were photographed in increasing order with rest photos only at the start (backup B4). The drift seen within single settings changes sign from setting to setting, and its average, −1 ± 1 mrad/s, is too uncertain to rule a steady drift in or out [Check]. (This is the plain mean of the ten drifts and its standard error. A mean weighted by their own error bars would be misleading, because the ten drifts differ far more than those error bars allow.) So our uncertainty does not cover such a drift.

**Next.**
- Take rest photos between the speeds and reverse the rotation. The Sagnac phase changes sign with the direction of rotation, a drift does not [Pascoli Sec. 2]. Taking the speeds in random order also helps.
- Count the fiber turns. N = 13 ± 1 is the largest uncertainty.
- Couple the light into a single fiber mode. At 640 nm the fiber can guide several modes (V = 5.6, backup B4), and the light goes into the fundamental mode only as far as the beam matches that mode. The manual works this out ("Optimum fiber coupling", p. 10): with a matched lens (f = 15.5 mm, waist 3.16 µm) up to 93 % can go into the fundamental mode, with the 8 mm lens of the setup at most 47 %. Coupling Gaussian beams into single-mode fibers is part of the lab's aim [Manual abstract]. It is possible with this fiber, but we found it very hard to align, and a longer-focal-length coupling lens would help. With mostly one mode the light in the fiber should be less sensitive to stress and temperature, a plausible source of the wander.

**B4 A steady phase drift would mimic part of the slope.** "Could something other than rotation change the phase during the run?"
- We photographed the speeds in increasing order, with rest photos only at the start. So ω and time increase together, and a phase that drifts steadily in time would look exactly like extra slope.
- **Top.** ω against time during the run: a staircase that only goes up.
- **Bottom.** Each photo's phase minus the fitted line of slide 8, against time, with a straight line through each setting's photos. Within single settings the phase drifts by −6.9 to +8.2 mrad/s, and 6 of the 10 drifts are significant (p < 0.05). Between settings the means wander by about 0.045 rad, the distance of the points from the line on slide 8.
- **Joint fit.** Δφ = aω + Dt + b, with t the mean time of each setting's photos, as a linear regression like slide 8 [Check. `sagnac_update/RESULTS.md` gives the correlation, `sagnac_update/sagnac.txt` the weighted version, 2.15 ± 0.20 and −0.9 ± 0.5]. It gives a = 2.1 ± 0.2 rad/(rad/s) and D = −0.8 ± 0.5 mrad/s, R² = 0.9991 (shown on the slide). The drift term is not significant (t test, p = 0.12), and the correlation between a and D is −0.99. With the first photo's time instead of the mean, D = −1.1 ± 0.5 and p = 0.07, the same conclusion. The data cannot tell a slope in ω from a drift in time, so we report the slope without D (1.84) and state that a steady drift is not covered by the quoted uncertainty.
- **A plausible source, not an established one (footer).** At 640 nm the fiber can guide several modes. Its V number is V = (2π/λ)·a·NA [Manual Eq. 9]. With a = 4.1 µm and NA = 0.14 the manual itself gets V = 5.6 at 640 nm, above the single-mode limit 2.405 [Manual, "Optimum fiber coupling", p. 10. The V-number section also gives 6.78 at 532 nm and 2.32 at 1550 nm. Prep Q12 asks exactly this question, and the answer is no]. Light in several modes is sensitive to fiber stress and temperature, because the modes exchange power and travel with different phases, and that can add a slowly varying phase that is different for the two directions. How much light goes into the fundamental mode depends on the coupling: up to 93 % with a matched 15.5 mm lens, at most 47 % with the setup's 8 mm lens [Manual p. 10]. Coupling into one mode is possible with this fiber but hard to align (slide 12).
- *If asked "both beams go through the same fiber, so why doesn't this cancel?"* For a single mode it does: the two directions see the same fiber, so stress and temperature cancel (reciprocity). With several modes, the power exchanged between modes along the fiber is not the same for the two directions, and the camera sees the whole multimode field, so the cancellation is not guaranteed. Fiber gyroscopes put a single-mode filter at the common port for exactly this reason (general knowledge, not in our documents).
- **How to separate it next time.** Take rest photos between the speeds, and reverse the rotation. The Sagnac phase changes sign with the direction of rotation, a drift does not. Sagnac himself measured this way, rotating in one direction and then the other [Pascoli Sec. 2].

**Other questions.**
- *Is this special relativity? There is no relativity on the slides.* Correct, the slides do not mention it. The derivation on slide 2 needs only that light moves at c in both directions in the lab frame, which is Einstein's second postulate. The effect is first order in v/c, and at that order the classical and relativistic derivations agree [Pascoli Sec. 3: Langevin notes it is "an experiment of the first order in v/c", unlike Michelson–Morley. Tartaglia: the same Eq. 2 "to lowest order in v/c"]. Corrections are of order (v/c)² ≈ 10⁻¹⁷ for our rim speed of about 1 m/s. The manual calls it "a fully relativistic effect" [Manual introduction]. Sagnac took it as evidence for an ether. Von Laue (1920) and Langevin (1921) showed it follows from relativity [Pascoli Sec. 3].
- *Does the refractive index of the fiber matter?* No [Schwartz Sec. 2, Prep Q10]. Here relativity does real work. In the fiber the light moves at c/n relative to the glass, and the glass moves with the table. With plain Galilean addition of velocities both beams would take the same time and there would be no effect at all. Before relativity, Fresnel's partial drag of light by a moving medium (measured by Fizeau) gave the right answer as an extra assumption [Pascoli Sec. 1.3 and 2]. Relativistic addition of velocities derives that drag and gives exactly the vacuum result 4NAω/c², without n. Schwartz: the medium "involves relativistic calculations", and the phase difference is independent of the material [Schwartz Sec. 2]. The Galilean statement is our own one-line calculation [Check].
- *Does it matter where the rotation axis is?* No. Only the enclosed area enters. Schwartz shows this by splitting the velocity into a rotation about the loop's center plus a constant vector, whose closed-loop integral vanishes [Schwartz Eq. 4, Prep Q8].
- *Why not measure the laser frequency instead of using λ?* The phase fixes the product λc. With a known frequency ν instead of λ, c = √(8π²NR²ν/a), with half the relative uncertainty [Analysis README].
- *Which fiber exactly?* The slides say SMF-28e+, the name on the datasheet printed in the manual and on the preparation sheet. The manual's own text says SMF-28e (slide 3 notes). Nothing in the analysis depends on it.
- *Why not the small off-center loop (task 4)?* We skipped task 4 on the instructor's instruction. The data and the deck are for the large loop only.
